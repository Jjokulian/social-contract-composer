How to Read a Contract

Read a contract from its intents down, test every reading against what actually happens, and settle disputes case by case among those competent to judge them.

What a social contract is

A contract between persons who implement it on a territory. They make it as they see fit, and it holds between those who accept it. A few cannot sign others into it, unless that too is agreed in the contract.

Only persons sign it and are its parties. An entity such as a court, a state or a police force exists only among the persons who signed the milli that made it: a social construct they made so that their system works better between them. For everyone else it is simply their construct, with no hold on them. Even within the milli it acts only through persons, in positions the signatories agree to. Treating such an entity as a party, or as binding those who never signed, is non-societas, the opposite of a partnership by consent.

The parties can agree on what breaching a grievous part costs, for example forfeiting properties or estates, exile, or loss of citizenship. The same parties can overrule it: they can re-admit the person and hand back their possessions. Consequences are set by the composition, so the same clause can carry different consequences in different societies.

Before you say “I do”

Any doubt you have about a contract, before you agree to it, can be tested in the open. Raise it as an issue. It is digested into a proposal the composer can test: which intents it serves, what it conflicts with, and which assumptions separate it from the contract. Everyone can see how each doubt was resolved.

Four rules of reading

  1. Remember what the intents are.

    The intents are what the contract is for. Every clause is a means to them and is read as one. When the wording of a clause seems to allow something that defeats an intent, the intent governs, and the reading is wrong.

  2. Deal with the manifest consequences.

    Ask what actually happens when a clause is applied one way or the other, and which intents that outcome serves or violates. A consequence that violates an intent is dealt with as a violation, whichever clause was used to reach it.

  3. Call out dramatization detached from reality, in either direction.

    Any rule attracts people who twist its words to their own aims: they exaggerate a situation to unlock a clause, or downplay it to block one, often by refusing to find out what would settle it. Both are stories detached from the actual case. Name them for what they are. What counts is the real condition of the people involved, never the story told about it.

  4. Settle disputes case by case, among the competent.

    When readings conflict over a real case, the case is explored by a guild of those competent to judge it, or crowdsourced at the level of competence the case requires. It is not settled by rule-lawyering, or by general opinion. What they find goes back into the contract as claims and evaluations and, where needed, as a new revision.

Example: fatal risk in the pro-pregnancy contract

The intents are to protect mothers at preference over their babies in the womb, and to protect babies. Before the baby is ready, a pregnancy may be aborted only when the mother is at fatal risk, now or on the trajectory she is on. That draws twists from both camps:

Twist one way

Dramatizing the mother’s state, to abort a pregnancy where the baby could have survived and thrived.

It defeats To protect babies. It rests on a story about her condition, not on her actual condition.

Twist the other way

Downplaying the mother’s state and the dire circumstances she is in, typically by refusing the diagnostics that could settle it, so that action comes too late and she dies.

It defeats To protect mothers at preference. It rests on not finding out her actual condition. A mother who dies is the gravest outcome there is, and it is not accepted at all.

The reading that holds: the mother’s survival comes first. Among the courses that secure it, the treating doctor, as a whole, with their competence and the methods they can make work, chooses the one that gives the baby the best chance. Where that is disputed, competent peers explore the case.

Writing with picos

When you raise a doubt or propose a clause, use the contract’s defined words as they are defined; hovering shows the definition. If you use a word in a strict sense that isn’t defined yet, say so: give its definition and the exact words that should refer to it. When the same word has two senses, each gets its own pico with its own words, so a reader can always tell which is meant. Mother thriving and baby thriving in the pro-pregnancy contract are an example.

What the composer shows you

Every word here is a pico in the composer’s own store, the same structure as the catalogue. Each meaning links the words it uses to their own definitions.

Pico
The smallest unit: a strictly defined word, which takes up no space. When a nano is written, it records which of its words refer to which pico, and that record never changes, so a newer pico never changes what an existing nano means. In software, a logical unit: one precise, self-contained definition, such as the fast inverse square root of Quake III.
Nano
A functional unit, written in words that use picos: an intent, a clause, a claim, a measure, an assumption, an influence or a consequence. In software, one behaviour, one requirement or one test, such as a projectile’s trajectory.
Micro
A micro-social-contract: a composable set of nanos stating concepts, relations and claims. It binds no one by itself, and it can name real things through picos, as a constitution names its founders. In software, a service, such as the real-time flow of projectiles by proximity.
Milli
An entire society, defined: a structure composed of micros, which can hold open roles and name real things. Persons sign it, and it is implemented as a demesne on a territory, or as a deme alone, in abstraction. In software, an application, such as Quake.
Demesne
A milli implemented on a segment of a coordinate space, such as an area of Earth. Demesnes nest: a demesne may be segmented exhaustively into others, or hold islands of others. In software, the implementation: the computers running an application and the instance they take part in, such as a multiplayer match of Quake.
Socioship
Having signed a milli together with other persons, and being bound by the contractual bindings they define. From Latin socius, a partner or companion: in Roman law a societas was a partnership made by consent alone. Citizenship ties belonging to a place; socioship ties it to the agreement between persons.
Signatory
A person who signs a milli. Everyone who has signed it is one of its signatories.
Signatoree
A person affected by a new signature: when a person signs a milli, every person already in the milli is a signatoree to that signatory or signatories.
Sociozen
A person who holds socioship: one who has signed a milli. The generic word for a member of a milli, from Latin socius, a partner by agreement.
Citizen
A person who stands under the order, the pax, that a deme provides: the relation to a stronghold, a power institution, a group of persons or a person. From Latin civis and civitas, the civic body under a city’s order. Not the generic word for a member.
Denizen
A person who lives within a demesne, whether or not they signed its milli. A milli binds only those who signed it. From Anglo-Norman deinzein, within.
Deme
Whoever a milli defines as the deme of its demesne. The deme of one layer is not the deme of another: demesnes within a demesne can have different demes.
Demot
A person who belongs to a deme and holds the rule its milli gives it. One sociozen can be a demot, an elector, a citizen and a denizen at once. From Greek dēmotēs, a member of an Attic deme.
Position
An attribute the signatories agree to give a person, such as judge, official or police officer, on account of which others are willing to do things. It exists only as that agreement: it is not a party, and it binds others only as far as the milli says.
Social construct
An entity such as a court, a state or a police force. It exists only among the persons who signed the milli that made it, as a virtual or social construct they made so that their system works better between them. For everyone else it is simply their construct.
Non-societas
Binding persons without their own consent, typically by treating an entity, the social construct of some signatories, as a party in the place of persons, or as binding those who never signed. The opposite of a societas, a partnership by consent.
Coordinate space
The frame a segment is given in: Earth in longitude and latitude, or any other body in its own frame.
Segment
A region of a coordinate space, on which a milli is implemented as a demesne.
Layer
What a viewer shows or hides on the Globe: every demesne at one nesting level, or every demesne of one milli. Hiding the layers above frees their visual cues for the layers below, where deeper demesnes appear as islands.
Intent
A purpose persons can grasp directly. Intents refine into narrower ones, and every clause is read as a means to them.
Clause
An agreement: who shall, shall not or may do what. A clause binds a role, which a milli fills.
Binding
What a clause binds its role to: work to carry out; a rule to abide by, whose breaches must be detected; or a liberty, where no one is bound to act.
Claim
A statement that a clause supports or hinders an intent, with the context it depends on: the clauses given, the parameter values, the assumptions, and the measures that would check it. As a rule of reasoning, one conclusion drawn from conditions that must all hold.
Coverage
How far the claims make an intent hold: claimed, by a sufficient claim or covered parts; thin, with only contributing claims; or a gap, with nothing.
Influence
What bears on what, recorded without asserting that it is true, including proxies that stand in for the real thing.
Disagreement
Two claims about the same clause and intent that reach different conclusions, shown with the context that separates them.
Operator
What a composition does to what it includes. It acts on pinned revisions and never changes what a contract or a word means, only what the composition holds; nothing it acts on is deleted from view. With M the provisions a composition holds, each operator is a union or a difference.
Abrogation
An operator: a composition takes a whole included contract out of effect, M ∖ A.
Derogation
An operator: a composition takes one provision of an included contract out of effect, M ∖ {n}.
Subrogation
An operator: a composition adds a provision into an included contract, M ∪ {n}.
Obrogation
An operator: a composition replaces one provision of an included contract with another, (M ∖ {n}) ∪ {n′}.
Maxim
A rule for resolving the conflicts no operator settles, stated by a milli in its order. What loses a conflict is set aside and its claims stop counting; with no maxim deciding, the conflict stays open.
Lex superior
A maxim: what comes through the base outranks what is added.
Lex specialis
A maxim: the provision declared special prevails over the general one.
Lex posterior
A maxim: what the later-composed contract brings prevails.
